Reading the results

Why can a party lead on seats but trail on votes?

Each council has two kinds of seats. Ward seats go to whoever wins each ward, and they are declared as soon as a ward finishes counting. Proportional (PR) seats are shared out only once the whole council has finished, to make each party's total seats match its share of the vote. Early on, a party that wins many wards can look ahead on seats even while it trails on votes. The PR seats correct this at the end.

What is a hung council?

A council is hung when no party holds more than half the seats. The parties then have to negotiate a coalition, or agree to support a minority government, to elect a mayor and pass budgets. Use the coalition builder on any council to see which combinations would reach a majority.

What is a voting district, and why does the percentage counted move in jumps?

A voting district is the area served by one voting station. Votes are counted at the station, checked, and captured by the Electoral Commission. Stations report at different times, so the share counted jumps as batches arrive. Rural stations and large urban ones often report later.

How are the projected seats in the coalition builder worked out?

For councils still counting, each party's projected seats come from its current vote share applied to the council's total seats. This is a rough guide, not a forecast: it assumes the uncounted votes look like the counted ones. Once a council finishes, the builder uses the official seat allocation.
